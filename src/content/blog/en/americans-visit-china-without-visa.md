Short answer: **yes**. US passport holders can stay in China for up to 10 days (240 hours) without a visa, using China's transit visa-free policy.

This isn't a loophole or a hack. It's an official Chinese government policy that's been running for years and was expanded in late 2023/early 2024. Here's exactly how it works for Americans.

## The basics

The **240-hour visa-free transit** policy lets US citizens enter China without a visa if you're transiting through (traveling from one country, through China, to a different country).

- **Duration:** Up to 240 hours (10 full days)
- **Cost:** Free. No visa application, no fee
- **Application:** None. Just show up at immigration with your passport and onward ticket
- **Eligible ports:** Most major airports, some train and sea crossings

## The one rule you need to know

Your flight **into** China and your flight **out** of China must involve **two different countries**.

**Works:**
- New York → Shanghai → Tokyo (US → China → Japan)
- Los Angeles → Beijing → Seoul (US → China → South Korea)
- San Francisco → Guangzhou → Bangkok (US → China → Thailand)
- London → Shanghai → Los Angeles (UK → China → US) — works for the return leg too

**Doesn't work:**
- Tokyo → Shanghai → Tokyo (same country on both sides)
- US → China → US (same country round trip)

This means you can use it on one leg of a round trip. Fly US → Japan, then Japan → China (stopover) → US. That second leg qualifies because you're going from Japan through China to the US.

## How to actually do it

### Step 1: Book flights with a China stopover

When booking flights to Asia, look for itineraries that route through a Chinese city. Many airlines fly through:

- **Shanghai (PVG)** — China Eastern, United, Delta, American
- **Beijing (PEK/PKX)** — Air China, United, Delta, American
- **Guangzhou (CAN)** — China Southern
- **Chengdu (TFU)** — Air China, Sichuan Airlines

Booking a "multi-city" itinerary (e.g., US → Shanghai, then Shanghai → Tokyo 5 days later) is the easiest way. Alternatively, ask your airline about extending a layover.

### Step 2: Check your specific route

Not all airport combinations allow travel to all regions of China. Use our [eligibility checker](/check) to verify your specific entry/exit port combination and see which provinces you can visit.

### Step 3: Prepare for arrival

At immigration in China, you'll need:
- US passport (valid 6+ months)
- Confirmed onward ticket leaving China within 240 hours
- Arrival/departure card (available at immigration counters)

No visa paperwork, no photos, no appointment. The immigration officer stamps you in and you're free for 10 days.

### Step 4: Get connected and set up payments

This is where most Americans get tripped up. China's digital ecosystem is very different from the US:

- **Internet:** Google, Gmail, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, and most US apps are blocked. [Get an eSIM with data](/esim) before you land.
- **Payments:** China is nearly cashless — most vendors use WeChat Pay and Alipay. [Set up mobile payments](/pay) before your trip or bring backup cash.
- **Apps:** [Download essential apps](/apps) before you arrive. WeChat is especially important — it's China's WhatsApp + Venmo + Uber combined.

## Cost comparison: Visa vs. transit

| | Tourist Visa (L Visa) | 240-Hour Transit |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | $185 (US citizens) | Free |
| **Application** | In-person at consulate or visa center | None — just show up |
| **Processing time** | 4-7 business days | Instant at immigration |
| **Duration** | 10 years / 60-day stays | 10 days per transit |
| **Restriction** | None (all of China) | May be limited by port zone |

For a stopover of 10 days or less, the transit policy is the clear winner. No $185 fee, no paperwork, no waiting.

## Best China stopovers for Americans

### Shanghai (3-5 days)
The Bund waterfront, French Concession for food and coffee, Nanjing Road shopping, day trip to the water towns. China's most foreigner-friendly city. English signage is common, and the metro system is excellent.

### Beijing (3-5 days)
The Great Wall, Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven, hutong alleys, Peking duck. The historical heavyweight. Less English than Shanghai, but taxis are cheap and the subway covers everything.

### Guangzhou/Shenzhen (2-3 days)
Cantonese food (dim sum capital of the world), modern tech city vibes in Shenzhen, day trip to Macau if your schedule allows. Good for food-focused travelers.

### Chengdu (3-4 days)
Giant pandas, Sichuan hotpot, tea houses, and one of China's most relaxed city vibes. Chengdu's Tianfu airport grants **nationwide** access, meaning you could even take a high-speed train to Xi'an to see the Terracotta Warriors.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need to show proof of a hotel booking?**
Not required at immigration, but it's good to have one ready in case they ask. Any booking confirmation on your phone works.

**Can I use this for business meetings?**
The transit policy is for tourism and transit. Technically, business activities require a business visa. In practice, having an informal meeting isn't going to trigger issues, but attending a formal trade show or signing contracts might.

**What if my outbound flight gets cancelled?**
Contact the airline and immigration. They're generally understanding about delays. The 240-hour clock is calculated from midnight after arrival, so you have a buffer built in.

**Can I re-enter China on another transit after I leave?**
Yes. Each qualifying transit is independent. You could transit through China twice on the same trip (outbound and return legs).
